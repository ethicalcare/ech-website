Cost of Private Home Care in British Columbia

By Ethical Care at Home · September 11, 2026

When families ask about cost, they usually need more than an hourly number. They need to know what kind of help is being priced, who will provide it and which changes could affect the monthly total.

Start with the short answer

The short answer

What does private home care cost in British Columbia?

There is no single price for private home care in BC. Cost usually depends on the kind of support needed, the qualifications of the person providing it, the number and timing of visits, the location and how quickly care must begin. Ask for a written explanation of what is included, any minimum hours and what could change the cost.

Why private home-care prices vary

Private home care is arranged directly between the family and the provider, so there is no single provincial rate. The same request can lead to different estimates because the work, schedule and qualifications may be different. A companion visit for meals and errands is not priced the same way as nursing care, awake overnight support or a short-notice start after hospital discharge.

Location and timing also matter. Evening, weekend and statutory-holiday visits may be priced differently. Some providers use minimum visit lengths, while others may charge for travel, supplies, an assessment or a cancelled visit. Ask about these details before comparing two hourly rates. A lower rate may not represent a lower total if the minimum hours or additional charges are different.

The care behind the price matters more than one hourly number

It is understandable to begin with what the family can afford. A useful estimate still starts with the person’s day. Which routines are difficult? Is help needed with meals, bathing, mobility, appointments or medication-related tasks? Does anyone need to remain awake at night? Is a spouse doing most of the care while managing health concerns of their own?

Those details help separate everyday home support from work that requires a registered nurse, licensed practical nurse or another regulated professional. They also show where a shorter visit may work and where rushing would create an unrealistic plan. The goal is a written estimate that matches the responsibilities, not an attractive number built around an incomplete description.

What belongs in a written home-care estimate

Ask the provider to list the hourly or visit rate, minimum hours, expected schedule and any assessment, travel, supply, evening, weekend or holiday charges. The estimate should explain which tasks are included, which tasks are excluded and which person is qualified to provide each part of care.

The agreement should also address cancellations, schedule changes and missed visits. Find out what happens if the regular caregiver is unavailable, if the family needs an extra visit or if the person’s health changes. These questions matter because care rarely stays identical for months at a time. A family should know how a new need will be reviewed before the bill changes.

Two home-care quotes are only comparable when the care is comparable

Give each provider the same description of the person’s needs and the same proposed schedule. Then compare the full arrangement. Look at who completes the first assessment, how caregivers are screened and supported, how backup is handled, how the family receives updates and when a nurse or another professional becomes involved.

Price is part of the decision, but clarity has value too. A provider who explains responsibilities, limits and possible added charges in writing gives the family something concrete to compare. Be cautious if the estimate is vague, if every service appears to have the same rate regardless of qualifications or if the family is asked to agree before the scope is clear.

Public and private support may share the same care plan

Some families receive publicly subsidized home and community care and add private support around it. The Province of British Columbia explains that publicly subsidized services are accessed through a health authority and are based on eligibility and assessment. Private services are arranged directly with the provider.

Write down what the public plan already covers before purchasing additional hours. A private visit may help with a morning routine, accompaniment, respite or family communication that is not covered at the preferred time. Keeping one schedule of all support helps prevent duplicated tasks and makes it easier to see which costs are truly filling a gap.

Home-care costs can change when care needs change

A family does not have to solve every future care need on the first day. It may be possible to begin with the routines causing the most strain and review the arrangement after everyone has seen how it works. Ask the provider how often the plan can be reviewed and who should be contacted if the person’s mobility, sleep, cognition or health changes.

Before care begins, calculate the likely weekly and monthly cost using the proposed schedule. Include minimum hours and known added charges. Then ask what a small increase in visits would mean for the budget. This gives the family a more realistic picture than multiplying one hourly rate by an uncertain number of hours.

A few things you can do now

A simple family checklist

  • Write down when help is needed and for how long
  • Note which tasks involve everyday support and which may need a nurse
  • Ask who will provide each part of care
  • Request a written estimate that explains what is and is not included

What to do next

Compare the whole arrangement, not only the hourly rate.

Write down the support needed, the likely schedule and any clinical tasks. Ask each provider for a written estimate that explains minimum hours, added charges and what could change the cost.

  • Ask each provider to price the same kind of help and schedule
  • Ask about minimum visit lengths and extra charges
  • Make sure you know when a nurse or another professional is needed
  • Ask what could make the weekly or monthly cost change
